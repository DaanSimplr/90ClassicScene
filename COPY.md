## 3. Mitsubishi 3000GT

**Title:** Mitsubishi 3000GT (GTO): Specs, History, Tuning and Body Kits | Shinyaso
**Description:** The Mitsubishi 3000GT, sold in Japan as the GTO: history, sourced specs for the VR-4 and its siblings, tuning information, body kits and documented builds.
**Target searches:** Mitsubishi 3000GT specs · Mitsubishi GTO history · 3000GT VR-4 tuning · 3000GT body kits

**H1:** Mitsubishi 3000GT

**Opening:**
The Mitsubishi 3000GT, sold in Japan as the GTO, was the car that got everything: twin turbos, all-wheel drive, four-wheel steering and aero that moved on its own. This page covers its history, sourced specs, tuning and body kits.

**History:**
Mitsubishi launched the GTO in Japan in 1990 and sold it abroad as the 3000GT. It was built around a 3.0-litre V6 from the 6G72 family, and the top version, the VR-4, added twin turbochargers and all-wheel drive.

It was a showcase for the technology of its time. The VR-4 carried four-wheel steering, electronically controlled suspension and active aerodynamics, with a front air dam and rear spoiler that changed position at speed.

In North America the car was also sold as the Dodge Stealth, built alongside it with different bodywork. A Spyder version with a retractable hardtop followed for the American market in the mid-90s. Production ran, with facelifts, until 2000.

**Tuning lead:**
The twin-turbo 6G72 responds well to the usual breathing and boost work, but the 3000GT asks more of its builder than most rivals: the engine bay is tight, the car is heavy, and the drivetrain has known limits. This section sets out what holds and what doesn't, with sources.

**Tuning detail:**

- **Weight comes first.** An early VR-4 weighed around 1,720 kg (3,800 lb), and the Spyder close to 1,860 kg (4,100 lb). Every upgrade below works against that. (source 1)
- **The turbos.** They are small, spin fast and sit in a crowded engine bay where heat builds up, and the labour to replace them is the expensive part. A stock pair should last 160,000 to 240,000 km (100,000 to 150,000 miles). Mitsubishi itself raised boost from 10 to 12 psi (0.7 to 0.8 bar) for 1994. (source 1)
- **The timing belt is not optional.** The 6G72 is an interference engine. The belt, tensioners and water pump are due every 97,000 km (60,000 miles), sooner if the history is unknown. (sources 1, 2)
- **The drivetrain is the real limit.** Both Getrag gearboxes, the five-speed to 1993 and the six-speed from 1994, have their own failure points at higher power, and replacements have become hard to find. Output shafts have twisted on hard launches, the synchros may last only 64,000 km (40,000 miles) if the car is shifted hard, and the gearboxes need regular fluid changes. The 1991 to 1993 VR-4 transfer case was the subject of a recall. CarThrottle's advice on boost: raise it at your own risk. (sources 1, 2)
- **The electronics.** In the US, the active aero and the electronically controlled suspension were dropped for 1995. On earlier cars, an ECS stuck in one mode or a flashing warning light usually means the control unit in the back has failed. (source 1)

**Tuning sources:**
1. CarThrottle, "Used car buyer's guide: Mitsubishi 3000GT VR-4", updated 10 October 2024: https://www.carthrottle.com/post/used-car-buyers-guide-mitsubishi-3000gt-vr-4/
2. Machines With Souls, Mike D'Ambrosio, "Mitsubishi 3000GT review", 18 July 2022, updated 5 September 2026: https://machineswithsouls.com/mitsubishi-3000gt-review/

**Body kits lead:**
The 3000GT changed face several times from the factory, so many owners start by swapping between years. Beyond that, the era's aero makers produced full kits for it.

## 4. Toyota MR2

**Title:** Toyota MR2: Specs, History, Tuning and Body Kits (AW11, SW20, ZZW30) | Shinyaso
**Description:** All three generations of the Toyota MR2: history, sourced specs for the AW11, SW20 and ZZW30, tuning information, body kits and documented builds.
**Target searches:** Toyota MR2 specs · MR2 SW20 history · MR2 SW20 body kits · MR2 Turbo tuning

**H1:** Toyota MR2

**Opening:**
The Toyota MR2 put the engine behind the driver at a price ordinary buyers could reach, across three generations: the AW11, the SW20 and the ZZW30. This page covers the history, sourced specs, tuning and body kits for each.

**History:**
The first MR2, the AW11, arrived in 1984: small, sharp-edged and powered by the 4A-GE twin-cam four. A supercharged version followed.

The second generation, the SW20, came in 1989 and is the one most people picture. It was larger and rounder, and in turbo form it used the 3S-GTE. Toyota revised it several times over ten years, and the revisions matter: suspension and tyre changes altered how the car behaves at the limit.

The third generation, the ZZW30, went on sale in 1999 as an open-top car, sold in Japan as the MR-S. It dropped the turbo for a lighter, simpler approach and ran until 2007.

**Tuning lead:**
The SW20 Turbo's 3S-GTE is the centre of most MR2 tuning, and it shares a family with the engines in the Celica GT-Four, so parts and knowledge cross over. The mid-engine layout brings its own questions: heat, access and what the chassis does when you add power. The AW11 and ZZW30 have their own well-trodden engine swap routes.

**Tuning detail:**

- **SW20: know the revision.** CarThrottle, quoting MR2 specialist Fensport, counts three: Rev 1; Rev 2, from December 1991 to November 1993, with chassis changes that made the car friendlier; and Rev 3, from November 1993 to June 1996, with rear bracing, revised engines and 550 cc injectors in place of 440 cc. Retro Rides adds that the early rear tie rods were too short, and a 1992 update lengthened them by 90 mm and changed the rear toe. A second CarThrottle guide, written for the US market, dates the main change to late 1993: retuned rear suspension, a thicker front anti-roll bar and wider rear tyres. (sources 1, 2, 3)
- **Snap oversteer is real.** Fensport confirms the reputation, especially for Rev 1 turbo cars. Retro Rides puts about 56 percent of the weight over the rear wheels. (sources 1, 3)
- **Turbo and boost.** The 1990 to 1993 cars have the second-generation 3S-GTE, the 1994 to 1999 cars the third. (source 4) The early CT26 turbo is good for about 16 psi (1.1 bar), around 280 bhp; the later CT20b spools faster and takes about 19 psi (1.3 bar), with up to 300 bhp possible. (source 2) Fensport puts the stock turbo and internals at 280 to 300 bhp on a safe 1 bar, and says the injectors run out quickly; beyond that, forged internals and a hybrid turbo take the car to 350 to 400 bhp. (source 1) The factory overboost fuel cut is set low, so it has to be dealt with before boost goes up, and Drifted advises against running leaner than 12.0:1. (source 4)
- **Heat.** The cooling system holds 15 litres and has four bleed points, and trapped air leads to overheating and a failed head gasket. The catalytic converter sits in the turbo downpipe and can cook at sustained high speed. After hard driving, let the engine idle for three minutes. (source 2) The coolant hose owners call the "Hose From Hell" can burst at around 160,000 km (100,000 miles), and the heater hoses above the fuel tank crack at their brackets. (source 1)
- **Ride height and alignment.** The standard car sits high. Fensport fits Tein springs, 37 mm lower at the front and 38 mm at the rear, with polyurethane bushes, and keeps the standard dampers. The cars go out of alignment easily. (sources 1, 2)
- **AW11: the supercharger.** The 4A-GZE uses a Roots-type SC12 blower with an electromagnetic clutch. Its efficiency falls to the low 50 percent range at higher boost, and the factory intercooler holds it back. The usual way to more boost is an oversized crank pulley or a smaller blower pulley, after which the engine runs lean at low rpm unless the fuelling is changed. (source 5)
- **Swap routes.** AW11: the 20-valve 4A-GE from overseas Corollas of 1992 to 1998, practical only in this chassis and needing revs to make power, or the 3S-GTE turbo engine, a swap known as the "Mk 1.5". SW20: fitting a turbo engine to a non-turbo car is relatively easy. ZZW30: the 2ZZ-GE fits directly, and the mechanical and electrical work is relatively easy. (source 6)

**Tuning sources:**
1. CarThrottle, Matt Robinson, "9 things you need to know before buying an SW20 Toyota MR2", updated 10 October 2024: https://www.carthrottle.com/post/9-things-you-need-to-know-before-buying-an-sw20-toyota-mr2
2. CarThrottle, "Used car buying guide: 2nd generation Toyota MR2 Turbo", updated 10 October 2024: https://www.carthrottle.com/news/used-car-buying-guide-2nd-generation-toyota-mr2-turbo
3. Retro Rides, Cliff Chambers, "Top tips for buying a used Toyota MR2 Turbo (1990-98)", 26 June 2025: https://retrorides.com.au/?p=10030
4. Drifted, "Toyota 3SGTE: the ultimate guide", 25 January 2023: https://www.drifted.com/3sgte/

**Body kits lead:**
The SW20's shape drew a wide range of kits, from subtle lip-and-skirt sets to wide-body conversions that change the car's whole outline.

## 5. Honda Integra

**Title:** Honda Integra: Specs, History, Tuning and Body Kits (DA, DC2, DC5) | Shinyaso
**Description:** The Honda Integra from the first generation to the DC5: history, sourced specs for the Type R and its siblings, tuning information, body kits and documented builds.
**Target searches:** Honda Integra specs · Integra Type R DC2 history · Integra DC2 tuning · Honda Integra body kits

**H1:** Honda Integra

**Opening:**
The Honda Integra proved a front-wheel-drive car could be a serious driver's car, most of all as the DC2 Type R. This page covers the Integra's history, sourced specs, tuning and body kits, generation by generation.

**History:**
The Integra began in 1985 as a sportier car above the Civic. The second generation, launched in 1989, carried the first production VTEC engine, the B16A.

The third generation arrived in 1993. In 1995 Honda added the Type R: a lighter, stiffer DC2 with a hand-finished B18C engine and a limited-slip differential. It is the version the model's reputation rests on.

The fourth generation, the DC5, followed in 2001 with the K20A engine and ran until 2006. In North America the Integra was sold under the Acura name, and the DC5 as the Acura RSX.

**Tuning lead:**
Integra tuning is engine tuning first. The B-series is among the most developed four-cylinder families there is, and the K-series that followed has become a common swap into the older cars. This section covers both, along with what the chassis wants: the Type R's factory changes are a good map of where to start.

**Tuning detail:**

- **What Honda changed for the Type R.** The engine was hand-assembled, with polished ports, lighter connecting rods, stronger pistons, reshaped intake valves and a larger throttle body. (source 1) The body got extra spot welds, thicker metal around the rear suspension mounts, less sound deadening and a thinner windscreen, plus a limited-slip differential. (source 2) Japanese cars had a better 4-2-1 exhaust manifold, a smaller catalytic converter, a higher final drive and closer first to third gears. (source 3)
- **Fuel and the ECU.** The engine was designed for 100 RON fuel in Japan, and on lower-octane fuel some cars can detonate unless they are remapped or fitted with a piggyback ECU such as Hondata. Common engine upgrades are cold-air intakes, uprated engine mounts, a baffled sump and adjustable cam gears. (source 3)
- **Known weak points.** The B18C uses some oil even in good health; up to 1 litre per 1,600 km (1,000 miles) is not unheard of, so check it often. (sources 2, 3) Warm it up before using the VTEC range. (source 1) The factory oil pump can fail on a car that has spent its life at the top of the rev range, and the crank pulley is another known weak spot. (source 3) Second and third gear synchros wear with track use. (sources 1, 2, 3)
- **Chassis.** Worn bushes are common, especially the rear trailing arm bushes; polyurethane bushes and Bilstein B6 dampers are the usual replacements, and the dampers are due by about 97,000 km (60,000 miles). (sources 2, 3)
- **Adding boost.** 8020 Automotive says a B18 makes 200 to 250 hp at the wheels without the engine being opened, and its fairly low compression suits boost. It puts the next steps at 250 to 300 hp (a stronger bottom end and head gasket), 350 to 400 hp (forged internals) and around 400 hp (sleeved cylinders). (source 4)
- **The K-series swap.** The K20 and K24 are commonly swapped into the DC2 Integra, chosen for power, tuning potential and aftermarket support. The swap needs specific engine mounts, swap driveshafts, a wiring conversion harness, a way round the ECU immobiliser (a bypass or a Hondata K-Pro or standalone ECU), a cable shifter in place of the factory linkage, a different exhaust manifold, a relocated radiator and a new clutch line. Estimates run from £4,000 to £8,000, or about $6,000. (sources 5, 6)

Japanese and export Integras wore different front ends, so the first body change many owners make is a front-end conversion. Beyond that, kits range from Type R replica parts to full aero from the Honda specialists.

## 6. Nissan Silvia

**Title:** Nissan Silvia: Specs, History, Tuning and Body Kits (S13, S14, S15) | Shinyaso
**Description:** The Nissan Silvia S13, S14 and S15, with the 180SX, 200SX and 240SX: history, sourced specs, tuning information, body kits and documented builds.
**Target searches:** Nissan Silvia S15 specs · Silvia S13 history · Silvia S14 body kits · Nissan Silvia tuning · 180SX specs

**H1:** Nissan Silvia

**Opening:**
The Nissan Silvia is the car drifting was built on. The S13, S14 and S15 gave buyers a light, rear-wheel-drive coupe with a turbo four, and tuners did the rest. This page covers the Silvia's history, sourced specs, tuning and body kits, with its 180SX, 200SX and 240SX siblings.

**History:**
The Silvia name dates back to a semi-handmade coupe of 1965, and it ran through several generations before the one that made it famous. The S13 arrived in 1988, first with the CA18DET and later with the SR20DET.

The S14 followed in 1993, wider and heavier, and the S15 in 1999, sold almost only in Japan. Production ended in 2002.

The same platform went abroad under other names. The 180SX was the hatchback sold in Japan beside the S13. In Europe the cars were sold as the 200SX, and in North America as the 240SX, with a larger non-turbo engine in place of the SR20DET.

**Tuning lead:**
The SR20DET is the reason there is so much to say here: a compact, strong turbo four with decades of development behind it. This section covers the engine, the common swaps for cars that never had it, and the suspension and steering changes that drifting made standard.

**Tuning detail:**

- **Know which SR20DET you have.** The engine changed with each generation: the S13 carried a Garrett T25G turbo, the S14 added variable cam timing and a larger T28, and the S15 got a T28R and 480 cc injectors. The factory ECU cannot be reflashed, so serious tuning means a standalone ECU. (source 2)
- **The head gasket goes first.** Tomei, which makes turbo kits for the engine, names the head gasket as the stock engine's first weak point and puts the stock engine at about 300 to 330 PS at best. On the factory gasket it advises no more than 1.0 to 1.1 kg/cm² (14.2 to 15.6 psi) of boost. A metal gasket allows 1.3 kg/cm² (18.5 psi), which Tomei also gives as the limit of the stock pistons; forged pistons take 1.5 to 1.6 kg/cm². (source 1)
- **Then the rockers, pistons and main caps.** The rocker arms are not shaft-mounted and can come loose at high rpm, which an aggressive rev limiter or an ignition-cut gear change makes worse; HP Academy recommends a fuel-cut limiter. It rates the stock pistons higher than Tomei does, at around 400 hp at the wheels, and says the cast main bearing caps give out from about 600 hp at the wheels. The 360-degree optical cam angle sensor is another known problem. (source 2)
- **The head sets the rev ceiling.** The standard head stops making power at around 7,500 rpm. For more, builders fit the SR20VE head, which HP Academy puts at a safe limit of around 8,500 rpm. (source 2)
- **What drifting made standard.** One parts maker's staged drift guide shows the usual order. First, for 150 to 300 hp: coilovers, steering-angle parts (longer lower control arms, tension rods and tie rods) for about 50 to 55 degrees of lock, a two-way limited-slip differential, a power steering cooler and a fixed bucket seat. Next, for 300 to 400 hp: steering rack relocation, a hydraulic handbrake, larger brakes from the Z32 300ZX and an oil cooler. Above that: a front-mounted intercooler, a standalone ECU, a bigger radiator and fans, and a roll cage. (source 3)
- **For cars that never had the SR20.** The same guide covers a turbocharged KA24DE in the 240SX, and 1JZ and GM LS swaps paired with a 350Z six-speed or 300ZX five-speed gearbox. (source 3)
- **Before buying a tuned one.** White exhaust smoke points to a turbo burning oil, clutch shudder to high-rpm standing starts, and wear on the inner edge of the rear tyres to wrong alignment. (source 4)

**Tuning sources:**
1. Tomei Powered (turbo kit maker), ARMS turbo kit guide for the SR20DET: https://tomei-p.co.jp/_2003web-catalogue/000_arms/product_arms_sr_guide_e.html
2. HP Academy (tuning school), Peter Kelly, "Everything you need to know about the SR20DET": https://hpacademy.com/blog/everything-you-need-to-know-about-the-sr20det
3. Chase Bays (parts maker and seller), "The drift S-chassis guide: S13, S14, S15": https://www.chasebays.com/pages/the-drift-s13-s14-s15-guide-180sx-240sx-silvia
4. Retro Rides, Cliff Chambers, "Top tips for buying a used Nissan Silvia S13-S15 (1988-2002)", 7 August 2025: https://retrorides.com.au/?p=10596

**Body kits lead:**
No car on this site has more kits. The Silvia was the canvas for the drift aero makers, and parts swap between the S-chassis cars, which is how front-end conversions like the "Sileighty" came about.

## 7. Toyota Supra

**Title:** Toyota Supra: Specs, History, Tuning and Body Kits (A70, A80) | Shinyaso
**Description:** The Toyota Supra from the Celica Supra to the A80: history, sourced specs, 2JZ-GTE tuning information, body kits and documented builds.
**Target searches:** Toyota Supra A80 specs · Supra MK4 history · 2JZ-GTE tuning · Supra A80 body kits

**H1:** Toyota Supra

**Opening:**
The Toyota Supra A80 is the car that made the 2JZ-GTE famous, and the engine returned the favour. This page covers the Supra's history from its Celica beginnings, with sourced specs, tuning and body kits focused on the A70 and A80.

**History:**
The Supra started in 1978 as a longer, six-cylinder version of the Celica. It became its own model with the A70 in 1986, which later gained turbocharged engines.

The A80 arrived in 1993. Toyota cut weight where it could and fitted the 3.0-litre 2JZ-GTE with sequential twin turbochargers. The car stayed on sale in Japan until 2002, by which time it had already left most export markets.

The name returned in 2019 on a car developed with BMW. That car is covered in the history here but is outside this page's detailed specs.

**Tuning lead:**
The 2JZ-GTE's reputation comes from how much power its standard bottom end is reported to hold. This section sorts the documented from the repeated: what the engine takes as standard, what changes first, and what the gearbox and the rest of the car need to keep up.

**Tuning detail:**

- **How much the bottom end takes.** The sources disagree. HP Academy calls 600 hp easy on stock internals and a sensible limit for an engine that is driven hard, with 800 hp possible once the main bearing caps, the first internal part to fail, are replaced. NZ Performance, a parts seller, is more cautious: 370 to 450 kW (about 500 to 600 hp) for a healthy stock engine with a proper tune. It adds that pump fuel runs out of octane before the engine runs out of strength, and that knock damages the rods and ring lands. (sources 1, 2)
- **Japanese and export engines differ.** Japanese cars have CT20 turbos with ceramic turbine wheels, which dislike high boost and ignition-cut limiters; US cars have CT12B turbos with steel wheels. Japanese cars also measure air with a MAP sensor and use 440 cc injectors, US cars an airflow meter and 550 cc injectors. (source 1)
- **The ECU.** The factory ECU cannot be reflashed, so tuning means a standalone or piggyback unit; a piggyback suits automatic cars. VVT-i, from mid-1997, limits how far the camshafts can be changed. (source 1)
- **The sequential system, and going single.** The handover from the first turbo to the second is the weak spot of the standard set-up, and its vacuum lines and actuators are prone to faults. The small turbos run out of breath at high power. A single turbo trades some low-rpm response for a much stronger top end, and needs a standalone ECU, larger injectors and usually a bigger fuel pump. (sources 1, 3)
- **Wear items.** Valve stem seals wear early and coil packs fail. (source 1)

**Tuning sources:**

**Title:** Nissan Skyline GT-R: Specs, History, Tuning and Body Kits (R32, R33, R34) | Shinyaso
**Description:** The Nissan Skyline GT-R R32, R33 and R34: history, sourced specs, RB26DETT tuning information, body kits and documented builds.
**Target searches:** Nissan Skyline GT-R R34 specs · Skyline GT-R R32 history · RB26DETT tuning · Skyline R33 GT-R body kits

**H1:** Nissan Skyline GT-R

**Opening:**
The Nissan Skyline GT-R came back in 1989 as the R32 and spent the next thirteen years as the benchmark. This page covers the GT-R's history from the first cars to the R35, with sourced specs, tuning and body kits for the R32, R33 and R34.

**History:**
The first Skyline GT-R appeared in 1969 and built its name in Japanese touring car racing. A second generation followed in 1973 and was gone within months. The badge then rested for sixteen years.

The R32 revived it in 1989, built to win Group A racing. It paired the twin-turbo RB26DETT straight-six with the ATTESA E-TS all-wheel-drive system, and it dominated the Japanese championship so completely that the Australian press named it Godzilla.

The R33 followed in 1995 and the R34 in 1999, each keeping the RB26DETT. R34 production ended in 2002. The GT-R that arrived in 2007 dropped the Skyline name and the straight-six; it is part of the history here but outside this page's detailed specs.

**Tuning lead:**
The RB26DETT was designed with racing in mind, and tuners have spent three decades finding out what that means. This section covers the engine's strengths and its documented weak points, and how the all-wheel-drive system shapes what a tuned GT-R can do.

**Tuning detail:**

- **Gaskets before boost.** Tomei, which makes turbochargers for the engine, names the head gasket as the RB26DETT's first weak point, with the intake manifold, exhaust manifold and throttle body gaskets close behind, and advises metal gaskets at the very least before boost goes up. With those, it puts the engine at 500 PS on its own T550B turbos at 1.1 to 1.2 kg/cm² (15.6 to 17.1 psi). (source 1)
- **The limits Tomei gives.** Stock pistons: about 500 PS; forged pistons allow 1.5 kg/cm² (21.3 psi). Connecting rods: a weak point, to be replaced above about 550 PS. Camshafts: change them above 1.4 kg/cm² (19.9 psi). Airflow meters: about 400 PS. The stock blow-off valves also leak boost at high pressure. (source 1)
- **Oil and revs.** Oil pump failure is a known RB26 risk, and an uprated pump is a priority once power goes up, along with an oil cooler. Option Motorsport found the crankshaft visibly shaking above 7,200 rpm, so Drifted advises a crank damper and a limit of about 7,000 rpm. (source 2) Nissan's own N1 engine for the R34 points the same way: it had uprated oil and water pumps, stronger pistons, rods and rings, and all-steel turbine wheels in place of the standard steel and ceramic ones. (source 3)
- **What ATTESA E-TS does with more power.** The system sends between 2 and 50 percent of the torque to the front wheels. An R32 can run almost entirely rear-drive; R33s and R34s always keep about 10 percent at the front. The controller reads its sensors 10 times a second on the R32, 100 on the R33 and 1,000 on the R34. Above about 680 Nm (500 lb-ft), the life of the factory front differential is in question, and the R32's has half the spider gears of the later cars. Re-stacking the transfer case clutches takes it to roughly 880 to 950 Nm (650 to 700 lb-ft). Aftermarket torque-split controllers and upgraded G-sensors change how the split is decided. (source 4)
- **R34 points.** The Getrag six-speed is known to jump out of first, and regular oil changes help it last. Super HICAS works above 80 km/h (50 mph), and some racers removed it. Vacuum lines, coil packs and airflow meters are age items, and the standard exhaust is restrictive. (source 3)

**Title:** Mazda RX-7: Specs, History, Tuning and Body Kits (FB, FC, FD) | Shinyaso

**Target searches:** Mazda RX-7 FD specs · RX-7 FC history · 13B-REW tuning · RX-7 FD body kits

**H1:** Mazda RX-7

**Opening:**
The Mazda RX-7 is the rotary sports car: three generations, all powered by an engine nobody else would commit to. This page covers the RX-7's history, sourced specs, tuning and body kits, with the most detail on the FD.

**History:**
The first RX-7 went on sale in 1978 as a light, simple coupe with a twin-rotor engine. The second generation, the FC, arrived in 1985 and brought the turbocharged 13B.

The FD followed in the early 90s. It was lighter than its rivals and used the 13B-REW with sequential twin turbochargers. Mazda kept developing it for the Japanese market through a series of revisions until production ended in 2002.

Behind the road cars sat Mazda's long commitment to rotary racing, which peaked with the 787B's win at Le Mans in 1991.

**Tuning lead:**
A rotary is tuned differently from a piston engine, and the advice that works on a 2JZ can ruin a 13B. This section covers what the engine needs to live, the single-turbo conversion many FDs end up with, porting, and the cooling work that comes before any of it.

**Tuning detail:**

- **Check the engine before anything else.** A compression test reading of 7 to 8 bar is good; lower readings point to worn apex or side seals, and a specialist rebuild costs around £3,000. Oil use of up to a quarter of a litre per 1,600 km (1,000 miles) is normal, so check the level at every fill, and change the oil every 4,800 km (3,000 miles). (source 1) HP Academy says the factory apex seals are good but brittle and can shatter, especially when carbon builds up in an engine that is never driven hard. The 13B is very sensitive to detonation, misfires and lean running, and running hot even briefly can wreck it. (source 2)
- **Cooling before power.** The coolant air separator tank can split, and for track use PistonHeads recommends an uprated tank and radiator. (source 1) 8020 Automotive recommends silicone in place of the rubber vacuum and coolant hoses, removing the expansion tank, and an aftermarket radiator and twin oil coolers; it also says heat makes the wiring harness brittle. (source 3)
- **The sequential twins.** The second turbo comes in at 4,500 rpm. Under-bonnet heat kills the solenoids that control the handover, so the turbos come in erratically, and it hardens the rubber pipes. (source 1) HP Academy calls the system prone to failure. (source 2) Many owners convert to one larger turbo, from around £3,500 in 2017. (source 1)
- **The ECU.** The factory ECU cannot be reflashed; HP Academy recommends a standalone ECU with staged injection and split ignition control. (source 2) PistonHeads names the Apexi Power FC as the usual first step. (source 1)
- **How much the engine takes.** The sources disagree. HP Academy says a 13B-REW makes upwards of 400 hp at the wheels before it needs opening up, with a street port, the right turbo and supporting work, and its own FD made 420 hp at the wheels on factory ports; beyond that it needs a stud or dowel kit, porting and rotor clearancing. 8020 Automotive claims 700 hp at the wheels with no internal changes, and puts the stock turbos at about 350 hp at the wheels on 15 psi (1.0 bar). (sources 2, 3)
- **Porting and seals.** In order of severity: street port, half-bridge, bridge, extended bridge (which means sealing the water jacket) and peripheral port, the last with a very rough idle and less low-down torque. The standard carbon apex seals suit naturally aspirated and moderately powerful turbo engines; ceramic seals shatter when they fail, and steel seals bend instead, though some wear the housings. Removing the oil metering pump means premixing oil with the fuel. (source 2)
- **Gearbox.** The fourth-to-fifth shift can be awkward, and most owners live with it. (source 1)

(source 4)

(source 5) DSPORT says Honda issued a service bulletin for 1991 and 1992 US cars. (source 3) The NSX has 23 coolant hoses, a weak coolant mix corrodes the aluminium heads, and the aluminium engine mounts can fail, which shows as a knock when the engine is revved. (source 5)

## 18. The 1990s

**Title:** 90s Japanese Sports Cars: The Golden Era, 1989 to 2002 | Shinyaso

**Target searches:** 90s Japanese sports cars · golden era of JDM · Japanese sports cars 1990s

**H1:** 90s Japanese sports cars: the golden era

**Opening:**
The golden era of 90s Japanese sports cars doesn't fit the calendar. It starts in 1989, when the R32 Skyline GT-R went on sale, and ends in 2002, the year the Skyline GT-R, Supra, RX-7 and Silvia all went out of production (sources 3, 4, 6, 17). This page explains what happened in between.

**H2: Where the money came from**
Japan's asset bubble ran from 1986 to 1991 and burst in early 1992 (source 1). Many of the cars on this site were designed in those years and reached showrooms around the turn of the decade: the R32 GT-R in 1989, the NSX and the Mitsubishi GTO in 1990, and the A80 Supra in 1993 (sources 3, 7, 8, 9).

**H2: The 280 PS agreement**
From the late 80s until 2004, Japan's carmakers held the advertised power of their home-market cars to 280 PS. It was a voluntary agreement, not a law (sources 10, 11). The cap shaped the whole era: with the headline figure fixed, engineers competed on everything else. [Read the full article: The 280 PS agreement]

**H2: The technology race**
Twin turbochargers, all-wheel drive, four-wheel steering, active aerodynamics and electronically controlled suspension all reached road cars in these years. The Mitsubishi GTO of 1990 had every one of them (source 7).

**H2: How the world found out**
The Skyline GT-R was built only in Japan, and Nissan sold it officially in just a few export markets: Hong Kong, Singapore, Australia and New Zealand from 1991, and Britain from 1997 (source 3). Racing put these cars in front of a wider audience, and so did the Gran Turismo games, first released in Japan in December 1997 with a GT-R and a Supra on the cover (source 12), and Initial D, a manga from 1995 and an anime from 1998 (source 13), along with imported magazines and videos.

**H2: The tuning scene**
The cars arrived with strong engines and a cap on their advertised power, and tuner shops treated that as an invitation. Top-speed runs, circuit time attacks and drifting each grew their own shops, magazines and heroes: Top Secret, whose founder Smokey Nagata built cars for top-speed runs from 1994 (source 14), and Option magazine and Keiichi Tsuchiya, who turned drifting into a judged sport (source 15). [Read more: A history of drifting]

**H2: Why it ended**
Buyers were moving away from sports cars (source 5), and in 2002 Japan's emissions rules tightened. Toyota says the Supra was dropped because meeting the coming exhaust emissions targets would have cost too much (source 5). The Skyline GT-R ended the same year, also because of emissions rules (source 16), and a Japanese report ties the Silvia's end to the same regulations (source 17). The RX-7's last edition, the Spirit R, went on sale in April 2002, and production ended that August (source 6).

## 21. Article: The 280 PS agreement

**Title:** The 280 PS Gentlemen's Agreement: What These Cars Really Made | Shinyaso
**Description:** How Japan's carmakers came to cap advertised power at 280 PS, which cars were reported to make more, and how the agreement ended.
**Target searches:** 280 PS gentlemen's agreement · JDM 276 hp limit

**H1:** The 280 PS agreement: what these cars really made

**Opening:**
For fifteen years, almost every fast Japanese car was advertised with the same power figure. The 280 PS gentlemen's agreement, often quoted abroad as the 276 hp limit, was never a law, and plenty of the cars it covered are reported to have made more. This article separates what is documented from what is repeated.

**Outline:**
- **H2: Where the cap came from.** The road-safety concerns of the late 80s and how the manufacturers' association responded.
- **H2: The first car at the limit.** Which car set the figure, and when.
- **H2: Advertised and actual.** Independent dyno tests and period reports for the GT-R, Supra and others, with each figure's source and how it was measured.
- **H2: What the cap changed.** Why engineers turned to torque, response, handling and technology.
- **H2: How it ended.** The first car advertised above 280 PS, and what followed.

**Body:**

#### Where the cap came from

In the late 1980s, Japan's carmakers agreed among themselves to advertise no more than 280 PS (276 hp) for cars sold at home. The sources do not agree on the year: Wikipedia gives 1988, CarThrottle and Jalopnik give 1989 (sources 1, 2, 5). They do agree that it was never a law. CarThrottle says it was not legally binding, and Wikipedia calls it voluntary self-restraint (sources 1, 2). The body named behind it is JAMA, the Japan Automobile Manufacturers Association (sources 1, 2, 3).

The reason usually given is road safety. CarThrottle and The News Wheel both put Japan's road deaths at more than 10,000 a year in the years before the agreement (sources 1, 3), and CarThrottle adds that the cap was meant to head off a horsepower war (source 1). The same package limited top speed to 180 km/h, a limit Wikipedia says still applies (sources 1, 2). None of our sources quotes a JAMA document that sets the cap out, so its exact wording is unknown.

#### The first car at the limit

The News Wheel says the agreement was timed to coincide with the launch of a new Nissan Fairlady Z (source 3). The R32 Skyline GT-R, which went into production in August 1989, was rated at the same 280 PS (source 7). By the mid-90s the figure was everywhere: the Mitsubishi GTO, the Honda NSX with a manual gearbox and the A80 Supra RZ were all rated at 280 PS in Japan (sources 8, 9, 10).

#### Advertised and actual

The one independent measurement in our sources is Doug DeMuro's 2015 test of his own 1989 R32 GT-R, standard apart from the exhaust, on a four-wheel-drive dyno at a tuning shop. It made 281 hp at the wheels, on three runs. DeMuro puts that at roughly 320 hp at the crank, using a rule of thumb that about 15 percent is lost through the drivetrain. That last figure is an estimate, not a measurement (source 5).

The carmakers' own export figures point the same way. The GTO was rated at 280 PS in Japan (source 8), while the 1994 3000GT VR-4 sold in North America was listed at 324 PS (source 11); CarThrottle says the export car made its higher figure with no mechanical changes (source 1). The Supra RZ was rated at 280 PS in Japan and the export Supra Turbo at 325 PS (source 10). For the GT-R, Wikipedia says enthusiasts have long believed the RB26DETT made closer to 320 PS by the end of production (source 12).

Other figures are repeated widely without a measurement behind them. CarThrottle mentions anything from 300 bhp for the R32 GT-R to more than 350 bhp for the R34, and around 300 bhp for the A80 Supra, and says these depend on which sources you believe (source 1).

#### What the cap changed

With the headline figure fixed, the numbers that moved were elsewhere. The R32 GT-R's torque was 353 Nm; the R34's was 392 Nm, with power still listed at 280 PS (sources 13, 14). DeMuro makes the same point about the R33: a new design, new suspension and a new all-wheel-drive system, and the same power figure (source 5). One engineering writer argues that the makers kept peak power at 280 PS while reshaping the torque curve beneath it (source 4).

The cap also had exceptions. CarThrottle says the makers' in-house tuning arms were exempt, and gives the Nismo 400R, rated at 400 PS, as an example (source 1).

#### How it ended

Honda launched a new Legend on 7 October 2004, rated at 300 PS (source 6). Honda's own announcement does not mention the agreement, but CarThrottle and other writers treat the Legend as the car that ended it (sources 1, 4). The News Wheel reports that in July 2004 Itaru Koeda, a former JAMA chairman, said JAMA had found no link between speed and road deaths, and called for the agreement to end (source 3). Wikipedia says the power limit was lifted in 2004, while the 180 km/h limit remains (source 2). Within a few years the figures had moved a long way: CarThrottle notes that the R35 GT-R of 2007 arrived with 473 bhp (source 1).

## 22. Article: RB26 and 2JZ

**Title:** RB26 vs 2JZ: Why Two Straight-Sixes Became Tuning Legends | Shinyaso
**Description:** The Nissan RB26DETT and Toyota 2JZ-GTE compared: how each was designed, why both take big power, and where each one's limits are.
**Target searches:** RB26 vs 2JZ · why is the 2JZ so strong

**H1:** RB26 and 2JZ: why two straight-sixes became tuning legends

**Opening:**
The RB26 vs 2JZ argument has run for thirty years and will outlive us all. Both are twin-turbo straight-sixes with iron blocks, both were built with more strength than their advertised power needed, and each has things the other does better. This article looks at why, starting with how each engine was designed.

**Outline:**
- **H2: Two briefs.** What Nissan built the RB26DETT for, and what Toyota built the 2JZ-GTE for.
- **H2: Inside the RB26.** Block, head, individual throttle bodies, and the documented weak points.
- **H2: Inside the 2JZ.** Block, head, the sequential turbo system, and why the bottom end has the reputation it does.
- **H2: Why both take power.** What the two designs have in common.
- **H2: Where each one stops.** Documented limits, with sources and the conditions behind each figure.
- **H2: So which one?** No winner declared: what each suits.

**Body:**

#### Two briefs

Nissan built the RB26DETT to win in Group A touring cars (sources 3, 4). Group A rules multiplied a turbocharged engine's capacity by 1.7. Wikipedia says the R32 GT-R was first planned with a 2.4-litre engine for the 4,000 cc class, and that Nismo then chose 2.6 litres and the 4,500 cc class, though it marks that last step as needing a citation (source 1). By our arithmetic, 2,568 cc times 1.7 is about 4,370 cc, inside the larger class. 8020 Automotive tells the origin differently, as the 2.0-litre RB20DET bored and stroked (source 3). The engine went on sale in 1989, in the R32 GT-R (source 3).

Toyota's 2JZ-GTE arrived in 1991 in the Aristo saloon and became the Supra RZ's engine in 1993 (source 2). HP Academy says Toyota's aim was an engine to rival the RB26, able to take far more power in motorsport without giving up durability (source 5).

#### Inside the RB26

A cast-iron block with an aluminium head, two camshafts and four valves per cylinder (sources 1, 3, 4). Bore 86 mm, stroke 73.7 mm, 2,568 cc (source 1). Instead of one throttle, it has six, one for each cylinder (sources 1, 4, 6). Two turbochargers work in parallel, not in sequence, with ceramic turbine wheels; Wikipedia's text calls them T25-type and its summary box T28-type (sources 1, 3). There is no variable valve timing (sources 3, 4, 6). Nissan rated it at 280 PS at 6,800 rpm and 36 kg·m (353 Nm) at 4,400 rpm (source 1).

The documented weak points start with oiling. On early engines, the part of the crankshaft that drives the oil pump was machined too narrow, which led to pump failure at high rpm; Wikipedia says the wider drive was fitted from the third series of the R32 on (sources 1, 3). The crankshaft vibrates between 7,000 and 8,000 rpm (source 1), and Drifted cites fibre-optic observation of it shaking above 7,200 rpm (source 6). 8020 Automotive adds weak pistons, misfiring at high rpm and failing airflow meters (source 3). Nissan's own N1 version replaced the ceramic turbine wheels with steel ones for strength (sources 1, 3).

#### Inside the 2JZ

Also a cast-iron block with an aluminium head (source 2). HP Academy adds that the block is closed-deck, with seven main bearings, a forged crankshaft and oil jets under the pistons (source 5). Bore and stroke are both 86 mm (sources 2, 5). The two turbochargers work in sequence, a system Toyota developed with Hitachi (source 2). Japanese cars had CT20 turbos with ceramic turbine wheels and 440 cc injectors; export cars had CT12B turbos with steel wheels and 550 cc injectors (sources 2, 5). Variable valve timing, VVT-i, arrived in 1997 (sources 2, 5). In Japan the Supra RZ was rated at 280 PS (source 7); Toyota documented 320 hp for North America and Europe (source 2).

HP Academy's list of weak points: the main bearing caps are the first internal part to fail on a standard engine; the changeover from one turbo to both is troublesome; the Japanese ceramic turbos can be fragile; valve stem seals wear early; coil packs fail; and the head can crack in serious use (source 5).

#### Why both take power

Both start with iron blocks (sources 1, 2). Wikipedia credits the RB26's reputation for strength to its iron block and forged internals (source 1), and 8020 Automotive notes its forged steel I-beam rods (source 3). HP Academy points to the 2JZ's closed deck and forged crank (source 5). Iron has a cost: DSPORT and Drifted both note how heavy the RB26's block is (sources 4, 6).

#### Where each one stops

Most of these figures come from tuners and magazines, and most do not say whether they are measured at the engine or at the wheels, or on what fuel. Where a source says, we say.

RB26DETT. Tomei, which makes turbochargers for it, puts the standard pistons at about 500 PS and says the connecting rods should be replaced above about 550 PS (source 8). 8020 Automotive is more cautious: the pistons often fail beyond 400 hp and the rods are good to around 450 hp (source 3). DSPORT says the standard turbos run out at 400 hp at the wheels, and that the block itself has supported more than 800 hp at the wheels (source 4). Drifted warns of failure at around 30 psi (2.1 bar) and 8,000 rpm (source 6).

2JZ-GTE. HP Academy calls 600 hp easy on standard internals with factory reliability, 800 hp possible with aftermarket main bearing caps, and 1,000 hp possible at a heavy cost in engine life; for a car driven hard and often, it suggests 600 hp (source 5). NZ Performance, which sells parts, is more cautious: 370 to 450 kW (about 500 to 600 hp) for a healthy standard engine with a proper tune (source 9).

#### So which one?

On these sources, the 2JZ-GTE's bottom end holds more power on standard parts, and its known problems are mostly around it rather than in it: the main caps, the turbo changeover, seals and coils. The RB26's limits come sooner, from the pistons, the oil pump drive and the crank's vibration at high rpm. In return, the RB26 brings a design made for Group A, six throttles and a shorter stroke, while the 2JZ brings more capacity and a closed deck. Which suits depends on the car around it and the power you want; the figures above are where to start.

#### Where aero came from

Body kits are older than the Japanese scene. Wikipedia traces them to American custom cars of the 20th century, and notes how many take their cues from racing cars (source 1). In Japan, the makers covered here came at it from several directions. RE Amemiya started as a rotary tuner and went racing in Super GT (source 2). Top Secret built its name on top-speed runs (sources 4, 5). VeilSide began with tuning parts and won its first prize at the Tokyo Auto Salon, the country's big tuning show (source 3).

#### The makers

This list covers the makers we could document from sources. Others often named alongside them are left out until we can.

VeilSide. Started in 1990 and based near the Tsukuba circuit (source 3). It first sold suspension and engine parts, took the Grand Prize in the tuned car category at the 1991 Tokyo Auto Salon, and won again in 1994 with its Supra "Combat" design (source 3). It received the Ministry of Transport's approval to make and sell aero parts in July 1995. Its best-known kit, the Fortune, replaces every body panel except the roof; Wikipedia lists versions for the FD RX-7, the A80 Supra and the NSX, among others (source 3). Wikipedia says its founder, Hironao Yokomaku, drew on the 1960s Batmobile (source 3).

RE Amemiya. Founded in 1974 by Isami Amemiya in Tomisato, Chiba, and known for its work on rotary-engined Mazdas; the RE stands for rotary engine (source 2). It raced a three-rotor RX-7 in Super GT from 1995, as the series' only rotary entry, and has run in the D1 Grand Prix since 2004 (source 2). In Initial D, the Takahashi brothers' FD and FC RX-7s wear RE Amemiya body kits (source 2).

Top Secret. Founded by Kazuhiko "Smokey" Nagata, who started out working on cars after hours at the tuner Trust; Wikipedia says the company became independent in 1991 and set up in Chiba (sources 4, 5). It has run speed trials since 1994, mostly with A80 Supras, and Nagata reached 358 km/h at the Nardò test track in Italy in a V12-engined Supra (sources 4, 5). That car wore Top Secret's own G-Force body kit, 33 mm wider than standard at the front and 55 mm at the rear (source 4). Gold paint is kept for the projects Nagata rates as his best (sources 4, 5).

ORIGIN Labo. Its DRIFT LINE kits, a front bumper, side steps and rear bumper in fibreglass, are made for the S13 180SX, the early S14 and the S15 Silvia (sources 6, 7, 8), and it also makes a full kit for the FD RX-7 (source 9).

Vertex. Kits for the S15 Silvia and the FD RX-7, including the EDGE wide body for the S15 and the Ridge wide body for the FD, are listed by retailers (sources 10, 11, 12).

Varis. Its wide body for the A80 Supra replaces the front wings, side skirts, rear wings and doors (source 13).

#### Styles, and how to tell them apart

The makers' own names and records point to four styles. Circuit and time-attack parts are made to work at speed; Top Secret says it has built cars for the street, drag strip, circuit, drifting and top speed (source 5). Drift kits are made for cars that spend their lives sideways, which is where ORIGIN Labo's DRIFT LINE gets its name (source 6). Top-speed cars are shaped for the long straight, as Top Secret's runs show (source 4). Show cars are built to be looked at, and the Tokyo Auto Salon is where VeilSide made its name (source 3). Wikipedia notes that some kits are made to improve a car's performance and others purely for looks (source 1).

JDM chassis codes look like noise until you know the system: BNR32, JZA80, FD3S. **Body:**

#### Why enthusiasts use codes

A model name can cover several very different cars. Toyota's own magazine calls the chassis code the quickest and most accurate way to identify a specific Toyota, especially where a name spans several generations, and says the AE86 became better known by its code than by the name on its boot lid (source 1). The code also separates cars that share a name: a buyer's guide to the R32 Skyline points out that only the BNR32 is the GT-R, and that fitting an RB26 engine to another R32 does not make it one (source 2).

A word of caution before the brand-by-brand part. Toyota has explained its system in print. For the other makers, we found no carmaker's explanation of what each letter means, so the sections below show the codes and the patterns they follow without claiming meanings nobody has documented.

#### Toyota

Toyota's magazine explains the AE86 this way: the first letter is the engine family, the A series; the second letter is the model family, the Corolla; and the two digits follow (source 1). Wikipedia adds that the 8 marks the generation, the E80 Corolla, and the 6 the version within it; the AE85 was the same car with the smaller 3A-U engine, and the AE82 the front-wheel-drive version (source 3).

The cars on this site follow the same rule. The MR2 went from AW11 with the 4A-GE, to SW20 with the 3S-GTE, to ZZW30 with the 1ZZ-FE; the Supra from JZA70 with the 1JZ-GTE to JZA80 with the 2JZ (sources 4, 5). In each case the leading letters match the engine family, and the letter after them stays the same for the model: W on all three MR2s, A on both Supras. That pattern is our reading of the codes, not Toyota's statement. The MR2 Wiki confirms that SW20 covers every second-generation MR2 with a 3S engine, while the American car with the 5S engine was the SW21 (source 6).

#### Nissan

Nissan has not published a key to its codes, and the R32 buyer's guide says as much (source 2). What the codes on this site do show is a pattern: the digits stay the same within a generation, while the letters in front change with the engine and the drivetrain. The first Silvia S13s, with the CA18 engine, are S13; the SR20 cars from 1991 are PS13; the 180SX is RS13 (source 7). The R32 GT-R is the BNR32, while other R32 Skylines carry codes such as HCR32 and ECR32 (source 2). The GT-R codes run BNR32, BCNR33 and BNR34 (source 8). The shared part, S13, S14 and S15 or R32, R33 and R34, is what enthusiasts use as shorthand for each generation.

#### Honda

Honda's codes on this site are DA6 for the Integra of 1989, DC2 and DC5 for the Integra Type Rs of 1995 and 2001, and NA1 and NA2 for the NSX (sources 9, 10). The NSX shows how messy this gets: a Japanese used-car catalogue lists the 3.2-litre NSX-R of 2002 under LA-NA1, while Wikipedia uses NA2 for all 3.2-litre cars (sources 10, 11). We found no source that explains the LA- in front.

#### Mazda

The three RX-7 generations are the SA22C, the FC3S and the FD3S (source 12). We found no source that explains the letters.

#### Mitsubishi

The GTO's codes split by engine: Z16A for the twin-turbo, four-wheel-drive car, Z15A for the naturally aspirated one (source 13). We found no source for the Lancer Evolution codes or their meaning.

#### Subaru

No Subaru on this site has a model page yet, and we found no source for Subaru's codes. This section waits until there is one.

## 25. Article: A history of drifting

**Title:** History of Drifting: From Japan's Mountain Passes to D1 | Shinyaso
**Description:** How drifting grew from a racing technique and late-night mountain runs into an international motorsport, and the cars that carried it.
**Target searches:** history of drifting · Japanese drift scene history · who invented drifting

**H1:** A history of drifting: from mountain passes to D1

**Opening:**
The history of drifting starts on Japanese race circuits in the 1970s, moves to the mountain passes at night, and ends up as a judged motorsport with a world following. This article traces how that happened, who drove it, and why a handful of rear-wheel-drive Nissans and Toyotas ended up at the centre of it.

**Outline:**
- **H2: A racing technique.** Kunimitsu Takahashi and the origins of driving past the limit of grip.
- **H2: The passes.** Street drivers, the touge, and Keiichi Tsuchiya.
- **H2: Print and video.** The magazines and videos that spread it.
- **H2: The cars.** The AE86, the Silvia and 180SX, and why they suited it.
- **H2: Becoming a sport.** The first judged events and the founding of D1 Grand Prix.
- **H2: Going abroad.** How drifting reached the rest of the world.

**Body:**

#### A racing technique

The name most often put at the start is Kunimitsu Takahashi. He began on motorcycles, and in 1961 became the first Japanese rider to win a motorcycle Grand Prix. After a crash in 1962 he moved to cars, racing on four wheels from 1965 (source 2). Wikipedia's article on him calls him the "father" of drifting; its article on drifting says he was the foremost creator of drifting techniques in the 1970s, though it marks that claim as needing a citation (sources 1, 2). The tyres of the time helped: Wikipedia notes that the bias-ply racing tyres of the 1960s to 1980s suited driving at high slip angles (source 1).

#### The passes

Keiichi Tsuchiya took an interest in Takahashi's technique and practised on Japan's mountain roads (source 1). He started racing in 1977 in the Fuji Freshman series, and in 1984 won six races there in an AE86 (source 3). He became known as the Drift King for drifting in races that were not about drifting (source 3).

#### Print and video

In 1987, several car magazines and tuning shops agreed to make a video of Tsuchiya's driving. Known as Pluspy, it became a hit and inspired many later professional drift drivers (source 1). It also nearly cost him his racing licence, because of the illegal street driving it recorded (source 3).

The magazines followed. Wikipedia says one of the first drifting contests was run by Carboy magazine in 1986 (source 4). Option magazine's founder, Daijiro Inada, organised one of the first events made only for drifting, the Ikaten, with Tsuchiya as a judge. Wikipedia's articles disagree on the year, 1988 or 1989 (sources 1, 4). Option's Video Option series began in 1988 (source 4). In 2003, Tsuchiya published the Drift Bible, a guide to the core drifting techniques (source 3).

#### The cars

The Toyota AE86, built from 1983 to 1987, became what Road & Track, quoted by Wikipedia, calls a cult icon inextricably interwoven with the earliest days of drifting (source 5). Nissan's Silvia took over as the series turned professional: in the D1 Grand Prix seasons of 2003 to 2005, the S15 was the most common car in the field (source 1), and the car brought over for the first drifting demonstration in America, in 1996, was a 180SX (source 1). What they share is rear-wheel drive, which drifting is done in almost exclusively; Wikipedia adds that a mechanical limited-slip differential is considered essential (source 1).

#### Becoming a sport

After years of amateur contests, Inada and Tsuchiya ran a professional-level contest in 1999 and 2000 (source 4). The first event of what became the D1 Grand Prix was held at Ebisu Circuit in Fukushima in October 2000, and counted as the first round of the 2001 season (source 4). From the second round, drivers went head to head in pairs, the format called tsuiso (source 4). Judges score speed, angle, showmanship and the line taken through a corner (source 1). Nobuteru Taniguchi won the 2001 championship on S-tyres, which were banned after that season because they are not road legal (source 4).

#### Going abroad

One of the earliest recorded drift events outside Japan was Option's demonstration at Willow Springs in California in 1996, with Inada and Tsuchiya among the drivers (source 1). The D1 Grand Prix itself left Japan for the first time in August 2003, for an exhibition at Irwindale Speedway in California (source 6). When D1 declined to start an American series, Jim Liaw and Ryan Sage set up their own: they announced Formula Drift at the SEMA show in 2003, and its first round ran in May 2004 (sources 6, 7). D1 went on to an exhibition at Silverstone in Britain in 2005 and to Malaysia and New Zealand in 2006 (source 4), and Formula Drift Asia started in Singapore in 2008 (source 7). Wikipedia now lists national drifting series across North America, Europe, Asia and Australia (source 1).
